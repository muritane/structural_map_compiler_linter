# Resolution Before Navigation

## A Standalone Framework for Capability Cartography, Concept Resolution, Witness Structure, Admissibility, and Map Fidelity

---

# Status

This document extends Capability Cartography.

Its purpose is to prevent a common mistake:

```text
maps are repeatedly mistaken for route planners
```

The framework argues that:

```text
navigation is not primary

planning is not primary

optimization is not primary
```

The primary problem is:

```text
resolution
```

Before a destination can be reached:

```text
it must first become mappable
```

---

# Abstract

Many capability frameworks eventually collapse into:

```text
current state

↓

goal state

↓

route search
```

This assumes that:

```text
the destination is known

the destination is well-defined

the destination occupies a stable location

the terrain has already been resolved
```

These assumptions are often false.

Before navigation can occur, a system must determine:

```text
what exists

what is being referred to

what distinctions matter

what resolution is required

which concepts possess witnesses

which concepts remain unresolved
```

The task is therefore not initially:

```text
How do I get there?
```

The prior task is:

```text
What exactly is "there"?
```

---

# 0. The Planner Mistake

A route planner assumes:

```text
map exists
```

A cartographer asks:

```text
what is the map?
```

A capability cartographer asks:

```text
what distinctions exist?

what transformations exist?

what supports exist?

what constraints exist?

what witnesses exist?
```

Navigation is downstream.

---

# 1. Resolution Before Reachability

Consider:

```text
student
→
engineer
```

This appears to be a transition.

But neither endpoint is fully resolved.

The map may reveal:

```text
student
```

contains:

```text
knowledge

credentials

experience

social position

institutional affiliation
```

Likewise:

```text
engineer
```

may expand into:

```text
civil engineer

software engineer

systems engineer

research engineer
```

The problem is not route finding.

The problem is insufficient map resolution.

---

# 2. Concepts Are Not Locations

Many labels are compressed regions.

Examples:

```text
engineer

civilization

dragon

cultivator

scientist
```

These are not coordinates.

They are unresolved map regions.

The cartographic task is:

```text
expand

decompose

differentiate

locate
```

before attempting navigation.

---

# 3. Witnesses

A witness is:

```text
an observed realization
```

Examples:

```text
elephant
```

possesses witnesses.

Examples:

```text
currently living dragon
```

does not possess known witnesses.

This distinction matters.

The question is not:

```text
can it be imagined?
```

The question is:

```text
what witnesses exist?
```

---

# 4. Witnessed Versus Unwitnessed

The framework distinguishes:

```text
witnessed

unwitnessed
```

A witnessed concept possesses at least one known realization.

An unwitnessed concept possesses none.

This does not determine admissibility.

It only determines observational status.

---

# 5. Grounded Versus Ungrounded

A concept may be:

```text
grounded
```

without being witnessed.

Example:

```text
dragon
```

may be partially grounded through:

```text
large reptiles

flight

predation

territorial behavior
```

The concept inherits support from witnessed substructures.

Grounding and witnessing are distinct.

---

# 6. Supported Versus Unsupported

Support concerns admissibility structure.

A concept is supported when known structures could potentially sustain it.

A concept is unsupported when no supporting structure is known.

This distinction differs from observation.

---

# 7. Unresolved Versus Impossible

A critical distinction:

```text
unresolved
≠
impossible
```

Unresolved means:

```text
current evidence is insufficient
```

Impossible means:

```text
the transition conflicts with the best available admissibility structure
```

The framework treats impossibility as provisional.

Maps remain accountable to reality.

---

# 8. Confidence Is Not Admissibility

Consider:

```text
energy conservation
```

This possesses extraordinary support.

However:

```text
high confidence
```

is not identical to:

```text
eternal certainty
```

The map records confidence.

Reality determines admissibility.

The two should not be conflated.

---

# 9. Concept Decomposition

Before many concepts become mappable they must be decomposed.

Example:

```text
dragon
```

may become:

```text
large body

flight

reptilian morphology

intelligence

fire projection
```

Each component may occupy a different region of capability space.

The map resolves the concept into its supporting structure.

---

# 10. Composite Concepts

Some concepts are composites assembled from witnessed regions.

Examples:

```text
dragon

warp drive

artificial general intelligence

perfect government
```

The map should indicate:

```text
which components are witnessed

which are grounded

which are unresolved

which are unsupported
```

rather than collapsing the concept into:

```text
real

or

fictional
```

---

# 11. Fidelity Before Navigation

A low-fidelity map encourages premature planning.

A high-fidelity map reveals:

```text
distinctions

supports

constraints

witnesses

confidence levels

resolution gaps
```

Only after fidelity is sufficient does navigation become meaningful.

---

# 12. The Cartographic Hierarchy

The framework proposes:

```text
distinction formation

↓

concept decomposition

↓

witness discovery

↓

grounding analysis

↓

support analysis

↓

admissibility resolution

↓

reachability analysis

↓

navigation

↓

optimization
```

Each layer depends on those above it.

Optimization is therefore among the latest operations.

Not among the earliest.

---

# Final Compression

A route planner asks:

```text
How do I get there?
```

A capability cartographer asks:

```text
What is "there"?

What supports it?

What witnesses it?

What grounds it?

What remains unresolved?

What distinctions are missing?

What resolution is required before navigation becomes meaningful?
```

The purpose of the map is not merely to find routes.

The purpose of the map is to increase fidelity until routes become well-defined objects.

Navigation is downstream of resolution.

Optimization is downstream of navigation.

Cartography comes first.
